# Response to Reviewer 4

This document responds only to the current Reviewer 4 report supplied for this revision. It is separate from the earlier editor/reviewer response draft and does not treat that prior revision as the current response set.

We thank Reviewer 4 for recognizing the software contribution while identifying the central limitation of the revision: the manuscript has emphasized higher-level interpretive capabilities more strongly than the validation evidence supports. We have therefore separated three claims that were previously conflated:

1. adapter fidelity: whether a browser adapter reproduces its reference package;
2. known-truth accuracy: whether an adapter recovers exposures in a published synthetic benchmark; and
3. tool triangulation: how much real-world exposure estimates and active-signature calls differ across the four packages.

The revised response and accompanying artifacts use the published synthetic benchmark for claim 2 and the real PCAWG cohort for claim 3.

## Major Comment 1. The central multi-tool value proposition is not directly demonstrated

We agree that the previous adapter-fidelity experiment established numerical agreement between each browser adapter and its corresponding local package, but did not directly demonstrate the scientific value of comparing multiple fitting tools. We have therefore added a new two-part, four-tool comparison and present it in a new Figure 4. The comparison evaluates both accuracy against known exposures in synthetic mixtures and tool-to-tool agreement in a real cancer cohort.

We revised the Methods to describe a common comparison framework for deconstructSigs, sigminer, SigProfilerAssignment, and MuSiCal. All outputs were aligned to the same ordered signature catalog; omitted signatures were assigned zero exposure; absolute outputs were converted to relative exposures; negative or non-finite values were set to zero; and retained exposures were renormalized after applying a common 1% relative-exposure threshold. We evaluated complete, unfiltered exposure vectors when measuring synthetic-mixture accuracy and applied the 1% threshold only when comparing active-signature calls. Catalog columns were normalized consistently for reconstruction, and the same numerical definitions were used for exposure correlation, per-sample L1 disagreement, active-signature concordance, and reconstruction cosine. Tool-specific optimization and signature-selection behavior was retained because these differences are part of what the comparison is intended to reveal.

For the known-truth analysis, we used the published synthetic SBS benchmark of Islam et al. (2022), as distributed with the Diaz-Gay et al. (2023) benchmark archive. We analyzed all 2,700 spectra at each of the archived 0%, 5%, and 10% noise levels with all four tools, for 32,400 fits in total. To make this full benchmark computationally feasible, we used the matched conventional R/Python packages targeted by the adapters: deconstructSigs 1.8.0, sigminer 2.3.1, SigProfilerAssignment 1.1.3, and MuSiCal 1.0.0. The revised manuscript now reports mean exposure cosine similarities against the known exposures of 0.9960, 1.0000, 0.9979, and 0.9996, respectively, at 0% noise; 0.9841, 0.9925, 0.9949, and 0.9990 at 5% noise; and 0.9672, 0.9712, 0.9909, and 0.9962 at 10% noise. Active-signature recovery was more sensitive to tool choice: at 10% noise, mean active-signature F1 against truth ranged from 0.532 to 0.924, and pairwise active-signature Jaccard similarity ranged from 0.355 to 0.866. These results show that overall exposure similarity can remain high even when the tools differ in which signatures they call active.

We also added a direct comparison of all four browser adapters using the same 38 PCAWG Lung-AdenoCA WGS SBS96 spectra and the same 67-signature COSMIC v3 GRCh37 catalog. In this real-world cohort, pairwise exposure correlations ranged from 0.803 to 0.953, mean per-sample L1 disagreement ranged from 0.350 to 0.907, and mean active-signature Jaccard similarity ranged from 0.186 to 0.694. The mean numbers of active signatures were 15.87 for deconstructSigs, 17.39 for sigminer, 4.13 for SigProfilerAssignment, and 11.21 for MuSiCal. Despite these differences, mean reconstruction cosine remained high for every tool, ranging from 0.982 to 0.994. The largest exposure differences involved SBS5, SBS40, SBS8, SBS4, SBS39, and SBS18.

These additions are now reflected throughout the manuscript. The Abstract summarizes the principal synthetic and PCAWG findings. The Methods include a new subsection, “Comparative four-tool benchmark,” defining the datasets, harmonization steps, and evaluation metrics. The Results include a new subsection, “Four-tool comparison on synthetic and real-world spectra.” New Figure 4A–B presents known-truth exposure accuracy and active-signature recovery across the three synthetic noise levels, whereas Figure 4C–F presents pairwise exposure correlation, per-sample disagreement, active-signature concordance, and the signatures responsible for the largest real-cohort discrepancies. We also added a Discussion paragraph explaining the scientific implication: similarly high reconstruction quality can conceal substantial differences in exposure allocation and active-signature calls, so the common adapter layer allows investigators to distinguish findings that are robust across tools from those that depend on the fitting method. Because the PCAWG cohort has no known biological exposure truth, we describe this portion of the analysis as tool triangulation rather than accuracy and do not infer that any one package is biologically superior. The previous runtime figure has been renumbered Figure 5, and its corresponding citations have been updated.

## Major Comment 2. The QC/reporting classifier is insufficiently validated

We agree that the threshold-sensitivity analysis in a single cohort does not validate the complete QC/reporting system. We have therefore revised the manuscript so that this component is no longer presented as a validated classifier. It is now described as a configurable, rule-based research-use review layer that organizes separable diagnostic evidence and identifies conditions that may warrant additional scrutiny.

In the Implementation section, we renamed “Quality-controlled review” as “Rule-based quality review” and revised the description of the four reporting modes. The previous label “standard quality-control passed” has been replaced in the manuscript by “no configured review cues detected.” This wording indicates only that none of the configured rules was triggered; it does not imply that a fit has passed a validated analytical or biological quality standard. The other modes—report with caveats, restricted interpretation, and not assessable—are likewise described as deterministic summaries of the configured review rules rather than calibrated classifications or confidence estimates. We also clarify that the default thresholds were selected as user-configurable research settings and are not community consensus thresholds or clinical decision cut points.

We revised the bootstrap description to distinguish conditional resampling uncertainty from validated interval performance. The reported intervals remain conditional on the observed spectrum, supplied signature catalog, fitting method, and filtering settings. Their empirical coverage has not been established across mutation burdens, correlated signatures, catalog misspecification, sequencing modalities, or tumor types. More generally, the sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, and clinical decision-making performance of the four reporting modes have not been evaluated.

The Results and Figure 3 caption now describe the Lung-AdenoCA exposure-threshold analysis as a descriptive numerical sensitivity analysis. Raising the exposure cutoff reduced the number of active signatures while only modestly changing reconstruction cosine, showing that the removed components made relatively small numerical contributions to reconstruction in this cohort. We now state explicitly that this finding does not establish that those components were biologically unimportant, confirm the correctness of the retained signatures, or validate the complete reporting system. We also replaced “quality-controlled report” with wording that describes the report as containing fitted exposures, structured review evidence, warnings, and provenance.

Finally, the Discussion now identifies the absence of classifier calibration as a limitation. It states that the reporting modes should be interpreted by examining the underlying evidence fields in the context of the supplied catalog, assay, cohort, and research question, and that they should not be used as clinical decision rules. We also identify evaluation across mutation burden, catalog misspecification, absent processes, correlated signatures, restricted-assay distortion, genome-build errors, and bootstrap interval coverage as future validation work.

## Major Comment 3. The NMF rank-selection result is not methodologically convincing

We agree that the original rank sweep, which relied on training reconstruction error and ended at the selected rank, did not provide adequate evidence for rank 6. We therefore replaced that analysis and revised the Exploratory extraction Methods, the public-cohort Results, Figure 3D and its legend, the internal-solver validation Results, and the Discussion limitations.

The revised rank-selection analysis evaluates ranks 2 through 8 using five deterministic sample-level folds. For each fold and candidate rank, signatures are learned only from the training samples using five seeded restarts. The best converged training solution is retained, its signature profiles are held fixed, and non-negative exposures are then refitted for the held-out samples. The primary selection objective is mean relative Frobenius reconstruction error on these held-out samples, rather than training error.

Restart reproducibility is quantified by exact one-to-one matching of components to maximize total profile cosine similarity, followed by the median matched cosine across restart pairs. We also report the mean pairwise adjusted Rand index of the samples’ maximum-exposure component assignments as a secondary clustering-stability diagnostic; it does not contribute a second tuning objective. Candidate ranks are eligible only when all five folds are available, at least 80% of runs converge, and median restart component stability is at least 0.90. Non-converged runs are excluded from fold summaries, ineligible ranks cannot be selected, and exact ties are resolved in favor of the smaller rank.

Rank selection is automatic. Among eligible ranks, the procedure chooses the smallest rank whose mean held-out error is within one standard error of the minimum. Rank 8 had the lowest point estimate of held-out error (0.0953), but rank 6 was within the one-standard-error limit (rank-6 mean 0.1143; selection limit 0.1146) and was therefore selected by the prespecified parsimony rule. All candidate ranks were eligible. In the subsequent 20-restart full-cohort fit at rank 6, all 20 runs converged, median component stability was 0.9648, and mean sample-clustering adjusted Rand index was 0.4525. We now describe rank 6 as a reproducible, cohort-specific exploratory choice, not as evidence that six is a universal biological dimensionality.

We also replaced the previous unmatched comparison with scikit-learn. The SDK and scikit-learn implementations were evaluated on the same input using rank 4, 12 paired restarts, identical positive initialization matrices for both factors in each pair, multiplicative-update optimization of the Frobenius objective, a maximum of 10,000 iterations, and a numerical tolerance of 1 × 10⁻⁷. Fitted components were matched by the same exact one-to-one maximum-total-cosine procedure. All 12 paired runs converged in both implementations; the median SDK-to-scikit-learn reconstruction-error ratio was 1.000006 and the median matched-component cosine was 0.9999999. Because convergence checks are implemented internally by the two APIs, their convergence flags were recorded separately rather than assumed to be identical. The revised manuscript therefore supports numerical agreement under matched objectives and procedures, rather than interpreting a lower unmatched reconstruction error as evidence of correctness.

## Major Comment 4. MAF-to-profile conversion is central but unvalidated

We agree that the previous manuscript did not adequately validate the MAF-to-profile converter. We therefore added a dedicated comparison with SigProfilerMatrixGenerator 1.3.6 and revised the Abstract, Methods, Results, Table 3, and Discussion. The converter remains described as experimental.

For single-base substitutions, we tested all 1,536 SBS1536 mutation contexts and their corresponding SBS96 classifications in both GRCh37 and GRCh38. mSigSDK and SigProfilerMatrixGenerator produced identical matrices in both genome builds. We also tested a 2,493-row MAF containing 19 samples. Both programs accepted the same 2,262 substitutions and produced identical SBS96 and SBS1536 matrices; both rejected the remaining 231 rows.

For double-base substitutions, mSigSDK generated all 78 DBS78 classes. SigProfilerMatrixGenerator accepted 77 of these classes from the MAF input, and all 77 agreed exactly with mSigSDK in both genome builds. SigProfilerMatrixGenerator rejected the TA>AT test event in both builds, so we identify this class as not compared rather than counting it as agreement.

For small insertions and deletions, we tested all 83 possible outputs accepted by mSigSDK when repeat or microhomology information is supplied. All 83 were assigned to the expected ID83 class. In a separate comparison using indels classified from their alleles and reference sequence by SigProfilerMatrixGenerator, 53 ID83 classes were represented in GRCh37 and 55 in GRCh38; none differed after the corresponding repeat or microhomology information was supplied to mSigSDK. We clarify that mSigSDK uses these annotations for multi-base indels and does not independently infer them from the reference sequence.

We separately ran 72 deliberately adverse rows covering wrong reference alleles, invalid chromosomes, missing and noninteger coordinates, N/ambiguous alleles, multiallelic alleles, undersized contexts, wrong variant types, unpaired SNVs, duplicate DNPs, overlapping three-SNV chains, invalid direct DNPs, complex replacements, and annotated or unannotated multi-base indels. The reconciliation table at `docs/manuscript/experiments/e9_maf_conversion_validation/data/maf-conversion-reconciliation.csv` records stable event IDs, build/profile, SDK counted/bin/skip reason, comparator counted/bin/status/error, case-level comparator units, differences, and the expected SDK decision. There were no comparator process errors: five adverse batches completed with SigProfilerMatrixGenerator's “more than 30% skipped” guard, two completed without a usable target matrix, and one completed with a matrix. These statuses, normalized comparator matrices, and logs are retained rather than discarded. The table contains 32 difference rows across 26 adverse case/build/profile units. For example, duplicate DNP rows are counted twice by mSigSDK but once by the comparator's case-level matrix, and the overlapping DBS chain can produce overlapping comparator pair bins; these are reported semantic differences, not counted as concordance.

We therefore separate the claims in the result JSON: `validExactConcordancePass: true`, `adverseReconciliationComplete: true`, `adverseExactConcordancePass: false`, and `referenceContextChecksPass: true`. The top-level `pass: true` means that the supported valid cases were exact, all adverse rows were accounted for, and reference-context checks passed; it does not mean that adverse rows were forced into exact agreement. In the 2,493-row example MAF, the SBS converter counted 2,262 rows and rejected 231: 230 because they were not single-nucleotide variants and one because its reference allele did not match the center base of the reference-derived context. Supplementary Table E reconciles these exclusions, and the generated reason counts are retained in `example-maf-validation-results.json`. The experiment establishes exact concordance only for the tested raw-MAF conventions, profiles, builds, and fixed edge cases. It does not validate every possible MAF normalization, indel annotation, reference assembly, or complex-variant convention. MAF conversion remains labeled experimental, and the response records the required Abstract/Results wording change: matrix generation should not be presented as having the same validation status as signature fitting. The strict-local smoke test remains evidence of execution and zero-egress behavior only, not matrix-generation correctness. The authoritative result is `docs/manuscript/experiments/e9_maf_conversion_validation/data/maf-conversion-validation-results.json`; normalized comparator artifacts are under `docs/manuscript/experiments/e9_maf_conversion_validation/data/comparator-artifacts/`. The revised manuscript now reports the number of mutations and mutation classes tested, removes the previous claim of exact agreement across all eight profile-by-build combinations, and states the remaining DBS78 and ID83 limitations directly.

## Major Comment 6. Data-residency language remains too broad

We agree with the proposed wording. The revised claim will be:

> User spectra and MAF rows are not uploaded to an mSigSDK-operated analysis service. Optional reference-context helpers may transmit genomic coordinates to public endpoints unless strict-local, no-egress mode is enabled.

The reproducible strict-local invocation is the SDK/runtime option `strictLocal: true`. In this mode, sample-specific mSigPortal retrieval, GDC lookups, live UCSC context lookup, cache persistence, and default identifier logging are disabled or blocked. Users must supply local spectra and either row-provided contexts or a bundled/offline context lookup table. The complete strict-local workflow was tested with fit, QC, NMF, report generation, and bundled-context MAF conversion; the recorded network log contains `networkRequests: 0`.

We will also revise Figure 1 so that “data never leave the device” is not interpreted as an unconditional browser security guarantee. The intended claim is limited to documented mSigSDK analysis paths under strict-local configuration.

## Major Comment 7. “Reproducible report” and “full provenance” overstate the evidence

We agree with the distinction between auditability, portability, and computational reproducibility. The report schema strongly supports auditability and portability through schema versioning, parameter capture, endpoint/timestamp recording, input and payload checksums, warnings, and evidence fields. A checksum does not archive or recover a resource that later disappears or changes.

We implemented this distinction in the report contract rather than only changing the adjective. `mSigSDKScripts/reports.js::createAnalysisReport` now emits a structured `reproducibility` object with separate `auditability`, `portability`, and `computationalReproducibility` statuses. `createReproducibilityRecord` enumerates the required immutable evidence: SDK artifact, adapter implementations, runtime/package artifacts, lockfiles or artifact hashes, local input hashes, signature-catalog payload, exact context order, fitting/filtering/bootstrap/QC parameters, RNG information, report-schema version, execution environment, archived test data, and rerun scripts. The schema at `schemas/msig.report.v0.3/report.schema.json` now requires every evidence slot, disallows a null top-level record, and validates the claim/evidence relationship: a computational claim requires structured hash-backed references with an immutable archive or embedded artifact, explicit fitting/filtering/bootstrap/QC parameter groups, and runtime/platform fields. The schema cannot verify that a remote archive will remain available, so the stronger claim remains evidence-supported rather than a promise that a checksum can recover a missing resource.

The default public report is now explicitly a `portable_schema_validated_analysis_record`; its computational-reproducibility status is `not_established` and it lists the missing evidence. A checksum remains a change detector, not an archive. A report may use the stronger `computationally_reproducible_analysis_record` claim only when callers supply all required immutable references. The schema enforces the shape and claim/evidence relationship, while the SDK builder also rejects placeholder hashes, non-archived references, incomplete fitting/filtering/bootstrap/QC parameter groups, and incomplete runtime/platform evidence. `npm run test:report-schema` includes a forged computational-claim fixture and confirms that schema validation rejects it. README and feature-reference wording now says that reports are easy to audit and cite, while rerun support depends on those references. This preserves the useful provenance fields and adds an enforceable boundary around the stronger claim without pretending that provenance alone guarantees recovery.

For the reviewer-4 benchmark, the experiment record retains the exact input/catalog/context checksums, package/runtime versions and options, seed policy, schema versions, and rerun entry points. The record also identifies the remaining limitation: the local comparator installation is described by observed versions rather than archived local-environment lockfiles, so this revision does not claim universal computational reproducibility for that run.

We added `docs/manuscript/experiments/e7_four_tool_comparison/reviewer4_benchmark/reviewer4-reproducibility-manifest.json` and embed it in `reviewer4-benchmark-provenance.json`. It is a recoverable, content-hashed manifest for the SDK source files, report schema, JavaScript lockfile, runtime/package manifests, exact input/catalog/archive artifacts, comparison scripts, context order, parameters, RNG algorithms/seeds, and execution environment. It records the current base commit and whether the working tree is dirty, rather than pretending that a dirty checkout is an immutable release. `npm run test:report-schema` now tests incomplete evidence, placeholder evidence, and a complete artifact manifest; only the genuinely complete manifest receives the computational-reproducibility claim.

## Major Comment 9. A common matrix shape does not establish semantic comparability

We agree and added a machine-readable semantic contract to the SDK adapter layer. `mSigSDKScripts/adapters.js` now exports `msig.adapter-comparison.v0.1` and attaches it to each prepared adapter input, each exact adapter provenance record, and `createInteroperabilityBundle`. The contract records the exact ordered context array, caller-supplied input units, complete-catalog signature membership, each tool's native exposure-unit interpretation, conversion to complete-catalog relative fractions, catalog-column normalization before reconstruction, the precise order of conversion, 1% filtering, and renormalization, and the treatment of unassigned, inactive, and package-omitted components. It also records the options deliberately fixed for the reviewer-4 comparison and the package-specific options/defaults that remain distinct.

The benchmark now consumes that same contract. Its raw accuracy metrics are calculated before the reporting cutoff; active-signature metrics are calculated after the explicit cutoff and renormalization. The contract states that package-native unassigned/unexplained components are not silently relabeled as catalog signatures. The comparison layer therefore standardizes the semantics needed for a fair evaluation while retaining the package-specific optimization objective, sparsity behavior, signature-selection logic, normalization internals, and post-processing. The synthetic known-truth panel uses and records its 78-signature contract separately from the 67-signature real-world adapter-fidelity contract; it no longer relies on an implicit catalog mismatch. `materializeAdapterComparisonExposure` records the actual output columns, omitted catalog columns, extra columns, and unassigned-like columns per tool, sample, and noise level before canonical-vector metrics are calculated. The benchmark output `docs/manuscript/experiments/e7_four_tool_comparison/reviewer4_benchmark/reviewer4-benchmark-provenance.json` embeds both contracts, and `scripts/smoke-adapters.mjs` now exercises omission, extra-column, and unassigned-column handling directly.

At 10% noise, mean active-signature precision was 0.446 for deconstructSigs, 0.447 for sigminer, 1.000 for SigProfilerAssignment, and 0.929 for MuSiCal. Supplementary Table D enumerates each package function called, every argument fixed for the comparison, and the arguments left at the package default.

The benchmark provenance now materializes per-tool omitted catalog columns and an explicit `unassigned` status/amount field, records the exact E2 input hashes and package options, and carries the full execution environment in the linked reproducibility manifest. The real-world and synthetic comparison contracts now use the same explicit seed, 104729; the generated four-tool result and the reviewer manifest agree. The browser adapter implementation itself carries the same contract in its prepared manifest and exact-run provenance, while E2 remains the separate test of browser-versus-local execution fidelity.
