# Response to Reviewer 4

This document responds only to the current Reviewer 4 report supplied for this revision. It is separate from the earlier editor/reviewer response draft and does not treat that prior revision as the current response set.

We thank Reviewer 4 for recognizing the software contribution while identifying the central limitation of the revision: the manuscript has emphasized higher-level interpretive capabilities more strongly than the validation evidence supports. We have therefore separated three claims that were previously conflated:

1. adapter fidelity: whether a browser adapter reproduces its reference package;
2. known-truth accuracy: whether an adapter recovers exposures in a published synthetic benchmark; and
3. tool triangulation: how much real-world exposure estimates and active-signature calls differ across the four packages.

The revised response and accompanying artifacts use the published synthetic benchmark for claim 2 and the real PCAWG cohort for claim 3.

## Major Comment 1. The central multi-tool value proposition is not directly demonstrated

We agree with the reviewer. The earlier adapter-fidelity experiment showed browser-versus-local agreement for each package, but it did not show what comparing four tools reveals scientifically. We added a two-part benchmark.

### Published synthetic benchmark: accuracy against known truth

We used the peer-reviewed synthetic SBS benchmark generated by Islam et al. (2022) and distributed with the Díaz-Gay et al. (2023) benchmark archive ([Islam et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36388765/); [Díaz-Gay et al.](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/39/12/btad756/7473371); [archive](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.24457114.v1)). We did not generate synthetic mutation spectra locally.

The archive contains 2,700 spectra generated from 21 reference signatures, with nine cancer-type groups and 0%, 5%, and 10% noise conditions. The executed subset contains one archived spectrum from each cancer-type group, or nine spectra per noise level. The archived SBS catalog contains 78 signature columns, which were passed unchanged to all four adapters.

The important correction is that exposure accuracy is now evaluated before the reporting cutoff. For each adapter, we first converted the complete output vector to relative fractions. We then calculated raw exposure cosine, RMSE, and MAE against the published true fractions. Separately, we applied the common 1% cutoff and evaluated active-signature precision, recall, F1, and active-signature concordance. This prevents the reporting filter from being treated as an accuracy-improving operation.

The raw mean exposure cosine values were:

| Noise | deconstructSigs | sigminer | SigProfilerAssignment | MuSiCal |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0% | 0.9983 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 | 0.9987 |
| 5% | 0.9879 | 0.9919 | 0.9996 | 0.9984 |
| 10% | 0.9719 | 0.9804 | 0.9894 | 0.9926 |

These results do not demonstrate that synthetic spectra produce large tool-to-tool disagreement. That is not what this controlled dataset is well suited to show: it contains high-burden mixtures generated from the same catalog used for fitting. We now use it only as a known-truth accuracy and sensitivity test. The 10% noise result is reported as evidence of degradation under the archived noise condition, not as evidence that the tools are interchangeable or that they should disagree substantially on this controlled benchmark.

### Real-world benchmark: tool triangulation

For the triangulation claim, we analyzed the same 38 real PCAWG Lung-AdenoCA WGS SBS96 spectra and the same 67-signature COSMIC GRCh37 catalog with all four adapters. After common catalog normalization, complete-catalog representation, relative-unit conversion, 1% reporting cutoff, renormalization, and common reconstruction metrics, the real-cohort comparison showed materially larger differences:

- flattened exposure correlations ranged from 0.803 for sigminer versus SigProfilerAssignment to 0.953 for deconstructSigs versus sigminer;
- mean per-sample L1 disagreement ranged from 0.350 to 0.907;
- mean active-signature Jaccard similarity ranged from 0.186 to 0.694;
- mean active-signature counts were 15.87 for deconstructSigs, 17.39 for sigminer, 4.13 for SigProfilerAssignment, and 11.21 for MuSiCal;
- the largest real-cohort exposure discrepancies were concentrated in SBS5, SBS40, SBS8, SBS4, SBS39, and SBS18.

Because the PCAWG cohort has no known exposure truth, these are disagreement and reconstruction results, not accuracy results. The scientific conclusion is now narrower and more defensible: mSigSDK enables investigators to identify conclusions that are stable or tool-sensitive across fitting implementations. It does not establish that one tool is biologically superior.

The combined analysis has a rerun entry point at `docs/manuscript/experiments/e7_four_tool_comparison/code/analyze-reviewer4-benchmark.mjs`. Outputs are in `docs/manuscript/experiments/e7_four_tool_comparison/reviewer4_benchmark/`, and the combined figure is `docs/manuscript/experiments/e7_four_tool_comparison/figures/figure-e7-reviewer4-benchmark.svg`; exact rerun support remains conditional on the archived inputs and environment references listed in the benchmark provenance record.

## Major Comment 2. The QC/reporting classifier is insufficiently validated

We agree. The existing threshold-sensitivity experiment evaluates only one evidence category and cannot calibrate the complete classifier. We will revise the manuscript and response language as follows:

- replace “standard quality-control passed” with “no configured review cues detected”;
- describe the four outputs as heuristic review tiers rather than validated quality classifications;
- state explicitly that sensitivity, specificity, interval coverage, and clinical decision-making performance have not been evaluated;
- state that the defaults are configurable review cues, not community or clinical cut points;
- avoid implying that a high reconstruction cosine establishes biological correctness.

The current QC threshold table and registry document the implemented cues, including burden, residual structure, threshold sensitivity, bootstrap stability, signature ambiguity, and catalog sufficiency. They do not constitute calibration. A complete synthetic calibration study spanning catalog misspecification, absent processes, collinearity, panel/WES distortion, genome-build errors, and bootstrap instability remains future validation work.

## Major Comment 3. The NMF rank-selection result is not methodologically convincing

We agree that the original training-error rank sweep was not sufficient. We replaced it with a reproducible rank-selection analysis and revised the manuscript figure and methods accordingly. In the current figure tree, the NMF rank-selection panel is Figure 3F; the reviewer’s Figure 3D numbering refers to the earlier panel arrangement.

The revised rank-selection objective is mean relative Frobenius reconstruction error on held-out samples. We evaluated ranks 2 through 8 using five deterministic sample-level folds. For each fold and rank, signatures were learned only from the training samples using five seeded restarts. The best converged training solution was retained, its signatures were held fixed, and non-negative exposures were refit for the held-out samples; the held-out error was then calculated without using held-out samples to update the signatures. Thus, the selection metric is held-out error, not training reconstruction error.

For each rank, restart reproducibility was quantified by exact one-to-one component matching that maximized the total cosine similarity across component profiles, followed by the median matched cosine over restart pairs. Sample clustering stability was also calculated as the mean pairwise adjusted Rand index of maximum-exposure component assignments, but was reported as a secondary diagnostic and was not used as a second tuning objective. A rank was eligible only when all five folds were available, the convergence rate was at least 0.80, and median restart component stability was at least 0.90. Non-converged runs were excluded from fold summaries; a rank that failed the eligibility requirements could not be selected. Ties were resolved toward the smaller rank.

Selection was automatic: among eligible ranks, we selected the smallest rank whose mean held-out error was within one standard error of the minimum mean held-out error. Rank 8 had the lowest point estimate (0.0953), but rank 6 was within the one-standard-error limit (rank-6 mean 0.1143; limit 0.1146) and was therefore selected by the prespecified parsimony rule. All ranks 2–8 were eligible. A 20-restart refit at rank 6 had 20/20 converged runs, median component stability 0.9648, and mean sample-clustering ARI 0.4525. These results support rank 6 as a reproducible analysis choice for this cohort, not as a claim that six is a universal biological dimensionality.

We also replaced the prior unmatched internal NMF comparison. The SDK and scikit-learn were run on the same input with rank 4, 12 paired restarts, the same positive W/H initialization matrices for each restart, multiplicative-update optimization of the Frobenius objective, a maximum of 10,000 iterations, and tolerance 1e-7. Components were matched after fitting with the same exact one-to-one maximum-total-cosine procedure. The median paired reconstruction-error ratio was 1.000006 and the median matched component cosine was 0.9999999; all 12/12 runs converged in both implementations. Because the two APIs implement their convergence checks internally, both convergence flags were recorded separately rather than treated as interchangeable; the numerical tolerance and iteration cap were matched. This comparison is therefore an objective-and-procedure-matched implementation validation, rather than an argument based only on one lower error value.

## Major Comment 4. MAF-to-profile conversion is central but unvalidated

We agree that the previous strict-local smoke test was not an external correctness validation. We added a fixed-input, edge-focused comparison experiment at `docs/manuscript/experiments/e9_maf_conversion_validation/` using SigProfilerMatrixGenerator 1.3.6 as an independent matrix generator. The run records the exact Python executable, comparator package and wrapper hashes, comparator command, reference volume, and SigProfilerMatrixGenerator reference checksum manifest. GRCh37 and GRCh38 references were installed and passed the comparator's checksum verification.

The fixture is deliberately a sparse spot-check rather than an exhaustive enumeration of every bin or normalization convention. It covers:

- SBS96 and SBS1536: six reference-backed SNP events per build, with pyrimidine-oriented and purine-oriented substitutions and build-specific contexts; across the combined two-build fixture, all six canonical substitution classes are covered. The 5-base contexts were checked independently against the fixed fixture context, the reference allele, and direct SigProfiler reference-sequence extraction before being supplied to mSigSDK through an offline lookup table.
- DBS78: an explicit DNP, a disjoint adjacent-SNV pair, and a second explicit DNP per build. These valid rows produce exact equality across all 78 bins, including zero bins, with matching event-level nonzero bins and totals.
- ID83: one single-base insertion and one single-base deletion per build. These produce exact equality across all 83 bins, including zero bins, and matching event-level bins and totals. Generic unannotated multi-base indels are now rejected as ambiguous rather than silently assigned to a repeat bin; annotated multi-base cases are exercised as adverse inputs and are not claimed as validated against this comparator.

All eight build/profile combinations (two builds × four profiles) passed exact integer matrix comparison, including every zero bin, total counts, per-sample conservation, trace partitioning, and isolated valid-event reconciliation. The valid fixture has 6/6 counted rows for SBS96 and SBS1536, 3 counted events from 4 source rows for DBS78, and 2/2 for ID83 in each build. The persisted valid-event table contains 34 event/build/profile reconciliation rows.

We separately ran 72 deliberately adverse rows covering wrong reference alleles, invalid chromosomes, missing and noninteger coordinates, N/ambiguous alleles, multiallelic alleles, undersized contexts, wrong variant types, unpaired SNVs, duplicate DNPs, overlapping three-SNV chains, invalid direct DNPs, complex replacements, and annotated or unannotated multi-base indels. The reconciliation table at `docs/manuscript/experiments/e9_maf_conversion_validation/data/maf-conversion-reconciliation.csv` records stable event IDs, build/profile, SDK counted/bin/skip reason, comparator counted/bin/status/error, case-level comparator units, differences, and the expected SDK decision. There were no comparator process errors: five adverse batches completed with SigProfilerMatrixGenerator's “more than 30% skipped” guard, two completed without a usable target matrix, and one completed with a matrix. These statuses, normalized comparator matrices, and logs are retained rather than discarded. The table contains 32 difference rows across 26 adverse case/build/profile units. For example, duplicate DNP rows are counted twice by mSigSDK but once by the comparator's case-level matrix, and the overlapping DBS chain can produce overlapping comparator pair bins; these are reported semantic differences, not counted as concordance.

We therefore separate the claims in the result JSON: `validExactConcordancePass: true`, `adverseReconciliationComplete: true`, `adverseExactConcordancePass: false`, and `referenceContextChecksPass: true`. The top-level `pass: true` means that the supported valid cases were exact, all adverse rows were accounted for, and reference-context checks passed; it does not mean that adverse rows were forced into exact agreement. The experiment establishes exact concordance only for the tested raw-MAF conventions, profiles, builds, and fixed edge cases. It does not validate every possible MAF normalization, indel annotation, reference assembly, or complex-variant convention. MAF conversion remains labeled experimental, and the response records the required Abstract/Results wording change: matrix generation should not be presented as having the same validation status as signature fitting. The strict-local smoke test remains evidence of execution and zero-egress behavior only, not matrix-generation correctness. The authoritative result is `docs/manuscript/experiments/e9_maf_conversion_validation/data/maf-conversion-validation-results.json`; normalized comparator artifacts are under `docs/manuscript/experiments/e9_maf_conversion_validation/data/comparator-artifacts/`.

## Major Comment 6. Data-residency language remains too broad

We agree with the proposed wording. The revised claim will be:

> User spectra and MAF rows are not uploaded to an mSigSDK-operated analysis service. Optional reference-context helpers may transmit genomic coordinates to public endpoints unless strict-local, no-egress mode is enabled.

The reproducible strict-local invocation is the SDK/runtime option `strictLocal: true`. In this mode, sample-specific mSigPortal retrieval, GDC lookups, live UCSC context lookup, cache persistence, and default identifier logging are disabled or blocked. Users must supply local spectra and either row-provided contexts or a bundled/offline context lookup table. The complete strict-local workflow was tested with fit, QC, NMF, report generation, and bundled-context MAF conversion; the recorded network log contains `networkRequests: 0`.

We will also revise Figure 1 so that “data never leave the device” is not interpreted as an unconditional browser security guarantee. The intended claim is limited to documented mSigSDK analysis paths under strict-local configuration.

## Major Comment 7. “Reproducible report” and “full provenance” overstate the evidence

We agree with the distinction between auditability, portability, and computational reproducibility. The report schema strongly supports auditability and portability through schema versioning, parameter capture, endpoint/timestamp recording, input and payload checksums, warnings, and evidence fields. A checksum does not archive or recover a resource that later disappears or changes.

We implemented this distinction in the report contract rather than only changing the adjective. `mSigSDKScripts/reports.js::createAnalysisReport` now emits a structured `reproducibility` object with separate `auditability`, `portability`, and `computationalReproducibility` statuses. `createReproducibilityRecord` enumerates the required immutable evidence: SDK artifact, adapter implementations, runtime/package artifacts, lockfiles or artifact hashes, local input hashes, signature-catalog payload, exact context order, fitting/filtering/bootstrap/QC parameters, RNG information, report-schema version, execution environment, archived test data, and rerun scripts. The schema at `schemas/msig.report.v0.3/report.schema.json` now requires every evidence slot, disallows a null top-level record, and validates the claim/evidence relationship: a computational claim requires structured hash-backed references with an immutable archive or embedded artifact, explicit fitting/filtering/bootstrap/QC parameter groups, and runtime/platform fields. The schema cannot verify that a remote archive will remain available, so the stronger claim remains evidence-supported rather than a promise that a checksum can recover a missing resource.

The default public report is now explicitly a `portable_schema_validated_analysis_record`; its computational-reproducibility status is `not_established` and it lists the missing evidence. A checksum remains a change detector, not an archive. A report may use the stronger `computationally_reproducible_analysis_record` claim only when callers supply all required immutable references. The schema enforces the shape and claim/evidence relationship, while the SDK builder also rejects placeholder hashes, non-archived references, incomplete fitting/filtering/bootstrap/QC parameter groups, and incomplete runtime/platform evidence. `npm run test:report-schema` includes a forged computational-claim fixture and confirms that schema validation rejects it. README and feature-reference wording now says that reports are easy to audit and cite, while rerun support depends on those references. This preserves the useful provenance fields and adds an enforceable boundary around the stronger claim without pretending that provenance alone guarantees recovery.

For the reviewer-4 benchmark, the experiment record retains the exact input/catalog/context checksums, package/runtime versions and options, seed policy, schema versions, and rerun entry points. The record also identifies the remaining limitation: the local comparator installation is described by observed versions rather than archived local-environment lockfiles, so this revision does not claim universal computational reproducibility for that run.

We added `docs/manuscript/experiments/e7_four_tool_comparison/reviewer4_benchmark/reviewer4-reproducibility-manifest.json` and embed it in `reviewer4-benchmark-provenance.json`. It is a recoverable, content-hashed manifest for the SDK source files, report schema, JavaScript lockfile, runtime/package manifests, exact input/catalog/archive artifacts, comparison scripts, context order, parameters, RNG algorithms/seeds, and execution environment. It records the current base commit and whether the working tree is dirty, rather than pretending that a dirty checkout is an immutable release. `npm run test:report-schema` now tests incomplete evidence, placeholder evidence, and a complete artifact manifest; only the genuinely complete manifest receives the computational-reproducibility claim.

## Major Comment 9. A common matrix shape does not establish semantic comparability

We agree and added a machine-readable semantic contract to the SDK adapter layer. `mSigSDKScripts/adapters.js` now exports `msig.adapter-comparison.v0.1` and attaches it to each prepared adapter input, each exact adapter provenance record, and `createInteroperabilityBundle`. The contract records the exact ordered context array, caller-supplied input units, complete-catalog signature membership, each tool's native exposure-unit interpretation, conversion to complete-catalog relative fractions, catalog-column normalization before reconstruction, the precise order of conversion, 1% filtering, and renormalization, and the treatment of unassigned, inactive, and package-omitted components. It also records the options deliberately fixed for the reviewer-4 comparison and the package-specific options/defaults that remain distinct.

The benchmark now consumes that same contract. Its raw accuracy metrics are calculated before the reporting cutoff; active-signature metrics are calculated after the explicit cutoff and renormalization. The contract states that package-native unassigned/unexplained components are not silently relabeled as catalog signatures. The comparison layer therefore standardizes the semantics needed for a fair evaluation while retaining the package-specific optimization objective, sparsity behavior, signature-selection logic, normalization internals, and post-processing. The synthetic known-truth panel uses and records its 78-signature contract separately from the 67-signature real-world adapter-fidelity contract; it no longer relies on an implicit catalog mismatch. `materializeAdapterComparisonExposure` records the actual output columns, omitted catalog columns, extra columns, and unassigned-like columns per tool, sample, and noise level before canonical-vector metrics are calculated. The benchmark output `docs/manuscript/experiments/e7_four_tool_comparison/reviewer4_benchmark/reviewer4-benchmark-provenance.json` embeds both contracts, and `scripts/smoke-adapters.mjs` now exercises omission, extra-column, and unassigned-column handling directly.

The benchmark provenance now materializes per-tool omitted catalog columns and an explicit `unassigned` status/amount field, records the exact E2 input hashes and package options, and carries the full execution environment in the linked reproducibility manifest. The real-world and synthetic comparison contracts now use the same explicit seed, 104729; the generated four-tool result and the reviewer manifest agree. The comparison normalizer and benchmark code both zero non-finite and negative exposure values before conversion, with `scripts/smoke-comparison-contract.mjs` covering that edge case. Because this reviewer benchmark used the available local package comparators, it is described as a comparison after explicit harmonization; it is not presented as evidence that the browser runtimes or package algorithms are semantically identical. The browser adapter implementation itself carries the same contract in its prepared manifest and exact-run provenance, while E2 remains the separate test of browser-versus-local execution fidelity.
